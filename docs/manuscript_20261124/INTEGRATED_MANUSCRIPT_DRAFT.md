# Geographically contingent reassembly of floral trait combinations on islands and their association with potential pollinator diets

Working manuscript, 24 November 2026. **Exploratory observational study; not submission-ready.** Numerical results are source-verified. A targeted literature comparison and five reproducible aggregate-data draft figures are now available; the complete bibliography, final methods and journal figure typesetting remain unfinished. This draft uses the global botanical data as the primary population and gives the independently compiled animal analyses a central Results section, rather than treating them as proof of the botanical mechanism.

## Abstract

Island floras are often expected to exhibit reduced floral specialization as pollinator assemblages become impoverished. Yet the functional consequences of isolation may depend on the composition, rather than simply the number, of potential interaction partners. We examined geographical reassembly of floral trait combinations in a global botanical frame of 4,505 island floras and evaluated exploratory associations with independently assembled bee, bird and butterfly records. Among 2,324 floras with directly documented flower colour and form, the same combinations recurred even between islands sharing no accepted plant species or genera. Relative to candidate mainland species pools, the proportion of actinomorphic, deep-tubed species was 10.99 percentage points higher on tropical islands (666 floras), whereas the corresponding contrast was negative at northern high latitudes. The tropical excess in symmetry–depth covariance was predominantly associated with differences in plant genus composition. In an independent animal-data integration, the specialist-minus-partial nectar-associated bird coefficient for tubular flower form was positive across all 11 estimable, nested sensitivity panels. Bird-family-conditioned dietary permutations supported this association in several panels, but genus-conditioned permutations, plant-side taxonomy controls and native-status sensitivity did not establish robust functional matching. On identical jointly scored plant cohorts, depth and form differed in their taxonomy-conditioned residuals. These observations motivate a distinction between geographical assembly of potential partners and the resource-access traits that may govern their interactions. Floral reassembly on islands is geographically contingent; changes in realized pollination and adaptive floral evolution remain unverified.

## Introduction

### 1. From an island floral syndrome to geographically contingent trait combinations

Biogeographical processes influence the composition and diversity of island floras. A functional island biogeography framework already predicts changes in mutualist diversity, reproductive strategy and pollination-associated traits with isolation (Schrader et al., 2021). Comparative evidence also challenges universal floral miniaturization: island–mainland sister-taxon contrasts show context-dependent flower-size evolution (Hetherington-Rauth & Johnson, 2020). Within Malesia, floral trait occurrence varies with climatic and geographical gradients (Trethowan et al., 2024), and multiple reproductive and floral traits jointly predict island occurrence at the species level (Zell et al., 2025). The general premise that island filtering is trait-dependent is therefore established.

A distinct assemblage-level question concerns the joint distribution of traits rather than their marginal frequencies or a scalar measure of functional diversity. Consider binary indicators A for actinomorphy and D for a deep floral tube. An island may differ from its candidate source in the frequency of A, the frequency of D, and the tendency for these states to occur together in the same documented plant species. The identity J = E[AD] = E[A]E[D] + Cov(A,D) separates a joint-frequency shift from the contribution of the association between the traits. The two components must not be conflated. In particular, nominal additive trait diversity can change little while the frequency of a particular joint category changes. The relevant question is whether the direction of joint floral reassembly and its covariance component differ among geographical regions.

### 2. Assemblage composition as a source of trait associations

Reconstructing island trait spaces is not a new goal. Barajas Barbosa et al. (2023) related the functional diversity of the Tenerife native flora to biogeographical and diversification history. Song et al. (2026) compared structural, hydraulic and carbon-related traits in Chinese island and mainland tree assemblages and documented differences between species- and community-scale trait associations. These findings provide a direct precedent for distinguishing changes in trait values from changes in their associations across organizational scales.

Here we extend that question to documented pollination-associated categorical phenotypes across a global island-flora frame, with potential mainland species pools as comparators. Conditional on a plant genus G, the law of total covariance partitions Cov(A,D) into Cov(E[A|G],E[D|G]) and E[Cov(A,D|G)]. We use the island–source contrast in these components to distinguish the contribution of genus composition and between-genus differences in category frequencies from residual within-genus associations. This is an application of a standard statistical identity, not a new decomposition method or a reconstruction of evolutionary change. Repeated floral categories across taxonomically disjoint assemblages provide a complementary descriptive result, not evidence for evolutionary convergence beyond a null.

### 3. Potential partner assembly versus realized interaction matching

Local field evidence already demonstrates the importance of pollinator function and composition. Hiraiwa and Ushimaru (2024) linked pollinator functional diversity to measured trait matching and reproductive success across coastal networks. Ishii et al. (2025) related colour, symmetry and tube length to pollinator fauna in six geographically distinct plant communities, accounting for plant family composition. Abrahamczyk (2019) compared generalist bird-pollinated plants between islands and continents. Thus partner identity, dietary differentiation, resource-access morphology and plant taxonomic composition are established subjects, not novel concepts in isolation.

Our question is whether the geographically varying combinations identified in the botanical analysis are accompanied by corresponding variation in independently recorded potential partners. We contrast the associations of nectar-associated bird diet classes with recorded floral form and depth, and use plant-side and bird-side taxonomy-conditioned expectations to ask whether apparent correspondence exceeds what their observed incidence and taxonomic structure permit. Neither island-level co-occurrence nor a species-wide diet fraction establishes local visitation or pollen delivery. We therefore separate geographical occupancy, resource dependence and possible functional matching as conceptual filters, without treating the three as an identified causal sequence.

### 4. Study questions and contribution

We ask four questions:
1. Do documented floral trait combinations recur between taxonomically dissimilar island floras?
2. Does the direction of joint floral reassembly relative to candidate mainland pools differ among macroregions, and how much of the focal tropical covariance contrast lies between versus within genera?
3. Do independently compiled potential pollinator assemblages show dietary and resource-use differentiation in relation to island geography and floral composition?
4. Do observed floral–diet associations exceed conditional expectations under plant taxonomy/record quality and animal taxonomy, separately and jointly?

The empirical contribution is the combination of geographically contrasting joint floral distributions, explicit genus accounting of the tropical covariance contrast, and an independent but geographically restricted animal-association analysis with two-sided nulls. The first two questions concern the global botanical study; the latter two are exploratory mechanism diagnostics. These are retrospectively organized study questions, not a newly preregistered confirmatory test. We do not claim a universal syndrome, an unprecedented analytical identity, or demonstrated pollinator-mediated evolution.

## Materials and methods — evidence-linked outline

### Botanical frame and trait carriers
Retain the 4,505 accepted GIFT island floras as the botanical study population; report endpoint-specific eligible denominators rather than imposing a full-trait complete-case subset. Use high/medium species-Direct categories, with genuine same-species conjunctions for joint endpoints. A species-wide documented phenotype projected to a local species list is not a locally measured phenotype. Distinguish known native, known introduced, and unresolved origin; do not infer local origin from absence of a label.

### Functional recurrence and geographical architecture
For colour × form frequency distributions, report total-variation overlap as 1 minus TV distance. Contrast all island pairs, pairs sharing no accepted species, and strictly genus-disjoint pairs, certifying genus membership against the entire recorded flora. Do not equate nonzero overlap with above-null convergence. For symmetry × tube depth, estimate island-minus-candidate-mainland joint frequency. Candidate source regions are potential comparators, not ancestral populations. Use the archived geo5 and climate10 source definitions and exclude the complete focal island roster from source pools where specified. Distinguish raw joint-frequency contrast J from covariance C = J − E[A]E[D]. For each island and its candidate source, use the law of total covariance C = Cov_G(E[A|G], E[D|G]) + E_G[Cov(A,D|G)], with genus weights determined by the scored distinct-species roster. The source-relative covariance contrast ΔC is the difference of the corresponding between- and within-genus components. Report ΔJ and ΔC separately: a difference in the joint frequency may also reflect a change in the product of the marginals. Geographical block intervals, original geographical origin and LGM support tables remain in the main evidence ledger.

### Independent animal source integration
Use the original native-bee model and polygons, independent bird checklist matrices with diet and wing-shape traits, and source-positive butterfly occurrences linked to LepTraits. Retain the provisional nature of bird island matching and the incomplete, noncomparable butterfly observation process. Bee/GSHHG matching requires unique mutual polygon overlap of at least 90%; do not replace unmatched geography with centroid matches. Define the bird categories explicitly from Diet.Nect (nectar/pollen/exudate): zero, >0 and <50%, and >=50%. These are diet classes, not demonstrated effective pollination guilds. Record denominators and original archipelago membership for every comparison.

### Dietary differentiation and floral correspondence
Fit group-equal within-archipelago models with log1p bee and bird-category counts, island area, mainland isolation and scored plant name counts; source-relative panels also control source scored richness. The specialist-minus-partial coefficient is a contrast of slopes, not a substitution at constant total animal richness. Compare form-only, depth-only, and true common-species form-depth endpoints. Preserve historical public colour/form name-unit aggregates as a discovery cohort distinct from the protected original species-Direct dataset. Retain all nested coverage, origin and geographic sensitivities.

### Two-sided taxonomy and origin diagnostics
For the animal-side null, hold island-by-bird incidence fixed, permute dietary labels coherently within family or genus across species. In the plant-side null, hold island and candidate-source incidence fixed and shuffle measured states within genus × evidence-grade blocks; maintain joint states together. On original common cohorts, compare plant-only, bird-only and independently joint permutations; require all three positive-direction tests (maximum p as intersection-union statistic), Holm across qualified comparisons. Document that these are targeted exploratory tests with few independent groups. For taxonomy expectation use exact block-mean substitution and a linear residual decomposition. Decompose known-native restriction into island-selection, scored-count control and plant-roster terms; distinguish known introduced from unresolved origin. Fixed-design partial-identification bounds over unresolved-origin subsets are not sampling confidence intervals or identified causal estimates. Held-out comparisons target centered within-archipelago contrasts, not absolute unseen-island predictions.

## Results

### R1. Floral functional repertoires recur despite taxonomic turnover (Fig. 1)
In 2,324 island floras with at least five directly scored colour × form plant taxa, 2,699,326 pairwise comparisons gave mean frequency overlap 0.386901. Among 842,058 pairs without a shared accepted species, overlap remained 0.247183. Among 155,569 strictly genus-disjoint pairs, overlap was 0.214879. These results show recurrence of documented floral categories across different assemblages; they do not demonstrate convergence above the taxonomy-preserving null. [Evidence A]

### R2. The direction of joint floral reassembly depends on macroregion (Fig. 2)
Relative to candidate mainland plant pools, actinomorphic × deep-tube joint frequency was elevated by 10.989 percentage points in the tropics (666 islands; physical-block interval +8.958 to +13.313). The corresponding estimates were +2.580 points in northern midlatitudes (1,443 islands; +1.064 to +4.131) and −2.704 points in northern high latitudes (135 islands; −3.562 to −1.895). A uniform directional simplification is therefore not a sufficient description of the observed joint category contrast. Among 123 tropical animal-matched islands, the positive contrast persisted under both geo5 (+9.52 points) and climate10 (+9.41 points). This is a nested cohort sensitivity, not independent replication. In the tropical botanical cohort the marginal-corrected covariance excess was +4.01790 points, of which +4.05652 was associated with between-genus composition and −0.03862 with within-genus differences. Geological origin and LGM connectivity showed respective positive contrasts (+6.69 and +6.90 points), but their effects were not independently identifiable. [Evidence B–C]

### R3. Geographical differentiation of potential interaction partners (Fig. 3)
The independent bee/flower crosswalk comprised 234 unique geometries; 231 matched the historical public floral aggregate. The bird–bee dataset contained 38 provisional tropical candidate islands in seven archipelagos, six of which were informative in within-group analyses. Relative to bees, the mainland-isolation slope for all nectar-associated birds was +0.256; relative to nonnectar birds, +0.207. The contrasts reversed or weakened for nearby-land distances; exploratory group-resampling ranges included zero. The >=50% diet subset did not retain a positive relative-to-bee slope. These results motivate separating diet classes and distance definitions, rather than claiming birds replace bees as isolation increases. Supporting analyses of bird wing shape and butterfly host-use breadth are retained in Supplementary Fig. S3: HWI–occupancy correlations were positive in nine of ten candidate archipelagos for all birds, but there was no robust nectar-specific advantage; butterfly documented larval host-family breadth declined in some source masks, while wingspan did not show a consistent filter. Source completeness prevents interpreting the latter as local host loss or superior establishment of specialists. [Evidence D]

### R4. Bird dietary classes differ in their association with recorded floral form (Fig. 4)
Across 32 tropical candidate islands with at least five historical Direct flower name units, coefficients for tubular form were +0.123372, −0.063260 and +0.046922 for the >=50%, partial and zero nectar-associated diet classes, respectively. Their specialist-minus-partial difference was +0.186632. All 11 estimable nested geography/coverage/state-set panels had positive tubular and extended-access contrasts; ten with estimable group omissions retained the positive tubular sign. In the public Direct min50 cohort of 24 islands, the tubular contrast was +0.258510; the family-restricted diet-label test had one-sided p=.0005, BH q=.006 across all 24 exploratory tests. Three tubular and three extended-access panels passed family-null BH; no genus-null comparison passed. In the same 24-island cohort, split-diet prediction reduced held-out within-group tubular-form MSE from .0075335 to .0022541 versus a broad nectar category, improving five of five groups. However, the broader 32-island cohort showed worse prediction, so the predictive gain was conditional. Red/pink × extended and nominal Q did not show consistent positive correspondence. These are dietary association results, not confirmed local floral preferences or functional replacement. [Evidence E]

### R5. Two-sided taxonomy separates raw correspondence from residual trait specificity (Fig. 5)
The original protected form-only data reproduced the positive specialist-minus-partial point estimates across all ten target and ten source-relative panels. Yet none of 500 plant genus-grade permutation tests across the larger original form/depth family survived BH. On exactly 30 common islands and the jointly scored plant roster, geo5 source-relative contrasts were +.019803 for tubular form, +.310323 for deep tube and +.169000 for tubular-and-deep. Exact genus-grade expectations were +.113496, +.114743 and +.204551, respectively; the resulting residuals were −.093692, +.195580 and −.035551. Climate10 produced residuals −.074534, +.187680 and −.030217. Thus morphology and depth need not show the same relationship with animal diet. The four positive unadjusted dual-null tests concerned depth in the same all-recorded 30-island cohort under alternative candidate sources/outcomes and bird-family constraints; 0/160 comparisons passed Holm, and no bird-genus IUT passed. In the 20-island common native-supported subset, the positive deep residual nearly disappeared before restricting the plant roster (−.009395 geo5; −.014964 climate10), and literal-native residuals were negative. In the preceding 30-island cohort median unresolved-origin share was .538995. All 460 feasible endpoint-wise partial-identification ranges included zero. Consequently we cannot identify a robust native floral–diet effect or interaction-mediated explanation of the tropical macroecological pattern. [Evidence F]

## Discussion

### Geographically contingent joint floral reassembly

A single directional shift in floral specialization is not sufficient to describe the observed island–candidate-source contrasts. The geographically heterogeneous flower-size changes of Hetherington-Rauth and Johnson (2020) and the island occurrence/trait interactions of Zell et al. (2025) motivate moving beyond a single trait expectation. Here the focus is the joint symmetry–depth state: the tropical contrast is positive while the northern high-latitude contrast is negative. Symmetry and depth describe different aspects of flower architecture, and the radial/deep category is not inherently a measurement of generalism or specialization. Its excess is a geographically contingent assemblage pattern, not a demonstration that flowers have become more complex or specialized.

We distinguish the increase in joint frequency (+10.989 percentage points) from the excess covariance (+4.01790 points) on the focal tropical scored cohort. The first includes the product-of-marginals difference; the second isolates the association component. The covariance accounting attributes nearly all of that difference to the between-genus term (+4.05652 points, within-genus −0.03862). This provides a quantitative counterpart for floral traits to broader evidence that community assembly can restructure trait associations (Song et al., 2026). It complements island trait-space studies such as Barajas Barbosa et al. (2023) without estimating the historical processes they address. The genus terms depend on present-day documented species composition and within-genus trait frequencies: they are not causal effects of establishment or species turnover, and do not test within-species adaptation. The recurring category frequencies of species- and genus-disjoint floras likewise do not demonstrate excess convergence against a taxonomy-preserving null.

### What the independently compiled animal data add

Ishii et al. (2025) explicitly investigated plant-family contributions alongside pollinator fauna and floral characteristics. Our extension is to assess a particular joint floral pattern in a global candidate-source framework and to ask whether animal-associated signals persist when both assemblages' taxonomic composition is retained in conditional nulls. The local interaction evidence of Hiraiwa and Ushimaru (2024) is stronger for actual flower use and pollen transfer than the island-wide co-occurrence data available here. Our data broaden geographical comparisons but cannot replace these measurements.

Diet-class differentiation is a useful empirical lead rather than proof of an alternative pollination system. Specialist-minus-partial diet coefficients for the recorded tubular category were positive in eleven estimable nested public panels, and some exceeded family-conditioned bird diet-label expectations. This extends the *comparison* to independently compiled island bird and plant assemblages; it does not discover the distinction between specialist and generalist bird pollination, already discussed by Abrahamczyk (2019). In addition, the Diet.Nect fraction includes nectar, pollen and exudates: a partial diet is not equivalent to a demonstrated generalist pollinator. None of the stronger genus-conditioned and original two-sided tests establishes robust functional correspondence. In the common island cohort the tubular coefficient was smaller than the plant genus-grade expectation, while the deep-class residual was positive under both source definitions. The latter disappeared on restricting the islands to those with sufficient literal native records, even before changing the plant roster. The correct interpretation is a contrast between observational association and conditional evidence for specificity, not an animal-mediated causal explanation of tropical trait covariance.

### A working hypothesis linking assembly and possible interactions

We use *combinatorial floral reassembly* descriptively for changes in the frequency and covariance of documented floral states relative to a specified comparator. It is not a newly established syndrome. The mechanistic hypothesis is that geographical filters on both plant and animal assemblages alter the available combinations of potential partners, while resource-access traits may further constrain realized interactions. This distinguishes flight-associated occupancy, establishment resource requirements (including larval hosts), and potential flower use. The distance-sensitive bird-richness contrasts and documented butterfly host-breadth associations are exploratory motivation, not support for an identified sequence of colonization, establishment and mutualism.

A prospective test should first specify a candidate-source-relative floral combination and the expected local animal trait distribution independently of the observed floral effect. It should then distinguish (i) a shift in marginal floral frequencies, (ii) a shift in floral covariance or lineage composition and (iii) residual floral–animal correspondence beyond fixed-incidence taxonomy-conditioned nulls. The latter should be tested on independent, geographically qualified groups with reliable native provenance and direct local visitor morphology, visitation and pollen transfer. A null result at the final stage would not negate the descriptive plant assemblage pattern; it would constrain a particular proposed interaction explanation.

### Limitations and inference ceilings

The animal and plant analyses cover different, nested sets of islands and have unequal geographical support; actual animal absence, abundance, pollen transport and local trait evolution are not observed. Native status is incompletely annotated, and recorded traits may reflect nonrandom documentation. Taxonomy-conditioned expectations are statistical decompositions, not phylogenetic mediation. Candidate source comparisons do not reconstruct mainland ancestors. The all-recorded result should not be described as a verified native-flora response. The two-sided null family and unresolved-origin bounds explicitly delimit inference; no robust native sign or independent mechanism is identified. The global plant contrasts and geographically restricted animal tests address related but different estimands. The following predictions remain open: (i) diet composition improves prediction of floral access traits on independent archipelagos; (ii) matching persists under independent taxon and origin support; (iii) measured visitation and pollen deposition depend on resource-access matching conditional on visitor availability. These should be tested with independent data, not additional searches for favourable subsets.

### Conclusion

Island floral trait combinations are geographically reassembled rather than uniformly shifted along a single specialization axis. Much of the tropical covariance excess is associated with genus composition. Independently compiled animal data yield exploratory dietary contrasts, but the conditional analyses prevent attributing the global botanical pattern to demonstrated pollinator replacement. The contribution is a joint-distribution account of floral assemblage differences and an explicit two-sided diagnostic of a proposed interaction mechanism. This provides a testable connection between functional island biogeography and pollination ecology while preserving the distinction between potential partner composition and realized mutualistic function.
